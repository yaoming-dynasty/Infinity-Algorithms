# Infinity-Algorithms — Foundational Axioms, Version 1.0

We will adopt the five recommended axioms as the provisional formal foundation of Infinity-Algorithms. The goal is to preserve your central ideas—zero as the unified origin, infinity as latent potential, distinct identities, and $0(1)$ as the organizing principle—while respecting the established limits of set theory.

## The five adopted axioms

## A1

### Unified Origin

$$
\mathcal Z=\{0\}
$$

The representations $-0,|-0|,|0|,0$ reduce to one numerical value. The origin is the framework's canonical starting state.

## A2

### Universal Domain

$$
\mathcal U=\text{the class of all sets}
$$

The universal domain is treated as a proper class, not an ordinary set containing everything. This avoids the direct universal-set contradiction in standard set theory.

## A3

### Distinct Identity

$$
i\ne j\implies(i,x)\ne(j,x)
$$

Different identity labels remain distinct even when their state values coincide.

## A4

### Generative Potential

$$
T:\Omega\to\mathcal P(\Omega)
$$

The transition operator $T$ defines the permitted successor states. Infinite potential must be established from the behavior of these transitions.

## A5

### Return to the Structured Origin

$$
R(\mathcal U)=0(1)
$$

The reduction operator $R$ maps the universal domain to the structured origin. This is a proposed axiom whose meaning and consistency depend on the precise definition of $R$.

## 2. What we have established—and what remains open

- Established mathematically: the four zero representations reduce to zero under ordinary arithmetic.
- Defined by the framework: identity labels can persist independently of numerical state values.
- Constrained by set theory: the collection of all sets must not be treated as an ordinary set in standard foundations.
- Still to prove: that the transition system has unbounded potential.
- Still to formalize: the reduction operator $R$, particularly how it maps the universal domain to $0(1)$ without erasing distinct identities.

One important technical point: $T$ is a successor-set-valued operator, while $R$ is intended to act on a proper class. In a rigorous formalization, we must specify the appropriate class-function framework rather than assume that an ordinary set function can accept the entire universe of sets as an argument.

## 3. The next step: define $0(1)$ and $R$

I recommend defining $0(1)$ as a structured state rather than a numerical expression:

$$
\boxed{Z_1=(0,\mathbf 1)}
$$

Here, $0$ is the canonical origin and $\mathbf 1$ is the organizing principle. This notation preserves your intended interpretation without confusing it with $0\times1=0$.

Then define a reduction map that preserves identity metadata:

$$
R(i,x)=(i,Z_1).
$$

Under this illustrative definition, different identities reduce to the same structured origin while remaining distinguishable:

$$
i\ne j
\implies
R(i,x)\ne R(j,y).
$$

This provides a concrete model of your common-origin principle. It does not, by itself, prove that the entire universal class can be reduced by the same operation; that larger claim needs a suitable class-theoretic definition.
